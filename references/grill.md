# The grill

Step 1 of showrunner. The goal is five agreed lines and one sentence. Everything after this (track, board, build) is timed and judged against them.

## Contents

1. How to run it
2. The ledger file
3. The sequence (23 questions in five groups)
4. The gate
5. Questions that wait for later steps
6. Where each question comes from

## 1. How to run it

- Ask one question at a time. Each question comes with your recommended answer and the evidence for it: the brief, the real product, the director's past verdicts (`learnings.md`), the measured references from step 0.
- Look before you ask. If the product, the launch doc or a past ledger answers it, write the answer in the ledger as "resolved by looking" and move on. Only taste, judgment and direction questions reach the director.
- No menus. If two answers are close, recommend one and name the other in a clause. Directors refuse question menus (rule 27).
- Write the director's answer into `ledger.md` before you ask the next question. A grill that lives only in chat is lost (the Film B grill ledger lived in a plan file that no longer exists).
- Skip what does not fit, and say you skipped it. A 20 s product demo may have no obstacle (rule 10). A checklist is a recommendation the director can overrule.
- Stop when the gate in section 4 is met. Do not keep asking to look thorough.

For the want, gap, turn and cost of a person-led story, use your own story-craft checklist if you have one. Section 3 covers a product film on its own.

## 2. The ledger file

`<film>/ledger.md`. Append only. Newest row at the bottom.

```markdown

| # | Question | Recommended (evidence) | The director's ruling, verbatim | Date |
```

The verdict table keeps running through every later step. The director's words go in verbatim, typos kept. That record is what the next film learns from.

## 3. The sequence

### A. The job of the film

1. **Who is the one viewer, and what do they already believe or fear?** Name a segment, not "users". (Tap existing beliefs; Duarte: "Your audience is the hero.")
2. **What should that viewer do in the hour after watching?** One action. It sets the last frame and the end card. (Ask what that person took away; Bland's hook, demo, CTA.)
3. **Where does it play, how long, sound on or off, and what comes before it?** Event screen, LinkedIn, Slack, bulletin. Feeds autoplay muted, so the film must read with the sound off. (Glaido and Google both read muted.)
4. **What should the viewer feel at the last frame, and what is the register: held and quiet, or cut to a beat?** Use the references from step 0 as evidence. A craft name is a standard, not a mood (rule 11). (Josh Miller: "We're there to make people feel something.")

### B. The shift

5. **What is the old game and the new game, in two or three words each?** Then "what is", "what could be", and the new bliss in one line each. (Raskin: "naming that old game"; Duarte's contrast.) Glaido's version: "Every screen changed. The keyboard didn't."
6. **Why must we tell this, and do we believe it?** Lead with why, not what. (Fadell; Kapoor: "You can't really create hype for something you don't believe in.")
7. **Which pain does the viewer feel first, and how late can we start?** Plant the doubt, then cut to "right before the bear". (Fadell's "virus of doubt"; Wes Kao; Dicks: "you've started your story in the wrong place.") The opening must move in 2 to 3 s (rule 24).

### C. The turn

8. **What is the five-second moment: the instant the viewer's belief flips?** Everything else is context for it. (Dicks: "every story is about a singular moment.") Google's is 7.5 s in: the real kite and the simulated kite side by side, on the music lift.
9. **What does the viewer want by second 5, as something on screen?** For a product film, the user's own words being typed is the want (Google: "build a kite simulation that shows h..."). For a person-led film, name what the person wants in one line.
10. **Is the surprise both unexpected and inevitable? Which earlier frame plants it?** (Dicks.)
11. **What is the proof the viewer can read off the screen?** A result, a number, a before next to an after. Not a claim on trust. (Google's input-output pairs.)
12. **Is anything claimed that the product does not do, or under-claimed from a stale doc?** Check against the current product, both ways (rule 25). (Fadell: "The best marketing just tells the truth.")

### D. The end

13. **What is the last frame? Write it first. Is the opening its opposite?** (Dicks: "start at the end"; Stanton; Pixar 7.) Show the payoff on screen, never imply it (rule 4).
14. **What is the one line, and is it shown before it is said?** Prefer the product's own words or the launch headline over a tagline (rule 9). Check it against the premise (rule 8). (Stanton: two plus two, never four.)
15. **What is the star moment, the one thing they will remember?** (Duarte.) One shot of wonder.

### E. Stress tests before the gate

16. **Say the film in one sentence with one image in it. Could a stranger repeat it?** (The stranger test.)
17. **What is each scene's need? Which beat needs explaining?** Cut it (rule 3).
18. **Would you tell it this way at dinner, with no performance art?** (Dicks's dinner test.)
19. **What happens if we cut the first 20 percent?** (Dicks: "The shortest version of every story is the best version.")
20. **Could a competitor run this same cut?** What is specific to this product, these customers, this place?
21. **Is it big, funny or relatable to how the viewer actually works, and is any of it new or slightly controversial?** (Kapoor on the Figma Slides demo; Bland: "new and slightly controversial.") Optional for an internal film.
22. **Is a film the right format, and is polish the lever?** A plain walkthrough sometimes does as well. (Product Hunt guide: four weeks and up to USD 10K for a polished teaser.) Ask this early when the story is thin.
23. **Have we told it to someone unwashed by the project?** (Fadell on Jobs: "tell other people who were unwashed by it".) The step 3 cold-viewer test does this for real.

## 4. The gate

The grill ends when these six are agreed and written in the ledger:

1. **Viewer and next action.** "Small shop owners on LinkedIn; they open the product and describe one job."
2. **Old game to new game.** "Filing a request and waiting weeks" to "describing it and watching it run."
3. **The turn.** The five-second moment, as a frame: "the typed sentence becomes a working agent."
4. **The last frame.** What is on screen when the film ends.
5. **The one line.** Exact words, exact case.
6. **The film in one sentence.** With one image in it.

Read them back to the director as a block and ask for one yes. Do not move to music or boards without it. If the director edits a line, the edit is the line.

## 5. Questions that wait for later steps

These are shot-craft questions. They come after the gate, at the track and the board:

- Which route: one long transforming move, or a burst of short cuts around one hold? Never between.
- Which track by ear, and where is its stop? (Step 2.)
- Where is the one held moment, about 3 times the median shot, and the one real drop in the sound?
- In each frame, where does the eye land first? What moves in the background?
- Which parts are generated and which are drawn in code? All type, UI and logos are code.
- Do the end frames of each shot, laid out as stills, read as the film? (Murch.)
