# Rules from real verdicts

Every rule here came from a verdict a director gave on a real film. Each has the rule and why it holds. The two films behind rules 1 to 33 are described in SKILL.md: Film A, built in code and locked at USD 0, and Film B, generated before the story was agreed and abandoned. Rules 34 to 50 come from two later films, Film C and Film D, described in section 10.

Read this before the grill on every film, and again before you show any board or cut.

## 1. Story

**1. Agree the story in words and one picture before any build or spend.**
Every expensive failure began with building ahead of agreement. On a board a change costs minutes. After a build it costs a re-render or more generation money. On Film B the director asked four times, across two days, to stop executing and agree the script first.

**2. A viewer with no context must follow the film. Watch the whole cut as a viewer, not shot by shot.**
Film B scored 11 of 12 on its own gates and was still "not coherent". Frame quality does not count when the sequence does not read. The director's note was that it looked good at frame level and made no sense overall.

**3. Every scene states its need. Cut a beat that needs explaining.**
Symbolic devices (a paperweight, two printings in one frame) read as fantasy to a cold viewer.

**4. Show the payoff on screen. Do not imply it.**
The human moment the film is about has to be in frame, or the film has no ending. Film B's final frame, the person finally free to spend time with family, was missing.

**5. No invented protagonist the viewer has no reason to care about.** For a product film the protagonist is the viewer's own job and the product.
An invented customer needs setup the film has no time for. The director's whole note on one was "Who is this". The real product flow carried the locked film.

**6. For a product film, the real product flow is the story spine.**
The flow already has a problem, a turn and a result. It is also true. On Film A the spine was: arrive at the problem, write the spec, run it on examples, then a trial run.

**7. Show range, not just ease. Put the capabilities inside the one story, not in a separate reel.**
A film that shows only ease undersells the product. A separate capability card breaks the story. On Film A the tools were folded into the reasoning steps instead of a card of their own.

**8. No line may contradict the premise.** Check every line against the film's own logic.
A line saying a task "can wait", in a film about agents doing the work in the background, undid its own premise.

**9. On-screen words must be strong and specific. No taglines.** Prefer the product's own words or the launch headline. A question the viewer recognises ("What do you never want to do again?") beat a slogan.

**10. A story checklist is a recommendation, not a gate the director must pass.** A 20 second demo can skip the obstacle slot. The director overrode a missing-obstacle warning on the film they later locked.

## 2. Register and taste

**11. A craft name sets a standard, not a mood.** When a director names Fincher, Lynch or Apple, take the precision. Take the tone from the brief. On Film A, "be like Fincher" was first read as dread; the director meant the craft, and wanted the film peppy and pulsing.

**12. Take taste from masters and measured references, not from popular explainers.** Averaging popular work gives the median, and directors call the median out by name.

**13. Story, picture and music come together. Never layer them.**

**14. Go all out on the first pass.** A quick one-shot output reads as low effort even when its base is right. Film A's first 14-minute attempt was rejected for effort, and its base later won.

## 3. Look and brand

**15. Use the real product and brand, never a lookalike.** Production components, real buttons, the real gradient, real option cards. Hand redraws fall below the real thing, and directors see it at a glance. "Looks like a cheap rip-off" was the verdict on an early redraw.

**16. One thing moving and one thing to read per frame.** Cut clutter, decorative colour fields and parallel cards. A busy opening, a gimmicky blue field and a wall of tool names were each rejected on Film A.

**17. Alignment and finish are part of the verdict.** Check text placement, highlights and button finish before you show anything. Misaligned boxes and a title sitting too high were each called out.

**18. Generated footage must hold identity, props and physics, or the film must not depend on it.** Draw type, UI and cuts in code. Film B's generated footage morphed people and props (a woman turned into a man, a phone changed shape) and was never finished. The code film cost USD 0 and was locked.

**19. For photoreal people, aim for everyday realism, not glossy AI cinema. The look alone does not save a film.** Film B moved to a raw, everyday phone-photo look, and its first animatic in that look was still rejected.

## 4. Rhythm and music

**20. Keep a structure and tempo once they work. Never stretch a cut to fit a new track.** A 34 second re-cut of Film A lost to the earlier 19 second structure.

**21. A score written for the cut beats a stock track, even one the director picked.** Once a score fits, a swap is a downgrade until the director hears otherwise. "A vibe downgrade" was the verdict on a swap.

**22. Music must hit the picture. A sound described in words is not a sound that syncs.** Time it to measured beats and check the joins.

**23. Pauses and intonation make a voice. Keep voice-over minimal; let picture and sound carry the story.**

**24. The first 2 to 3 seconds must move.** A slow "old way" opening was the weak part of an early Film A cut.

## 5. Truth and accuracy

**25. Show what the product really does. Check every claim against the current product, both ways.** No over-claim (calling a trial run "live"). No under-claim from a stale doc either: Film A at first left out a memory capability the product had.

**26. Truth rules cover outcome and capability claims, not every UI state.** In a demo, polish the UI (ticks, glow, hatching) and do not argue over server flags. "This is just a demo, make it good" was the ruling on a "Connected" tick.

## 6. How to talk to the director

**27. Plain prose and one recommendation. No question menus.** A four-question menu was refused, and a second one later. Single recommended options were answered with a click.

**28. Your own reviewers do not stand in for the director's eye.** Gate scores and model "stranger retells" passed work that was then rejected on sight. Show the real frame or cut early.

## 7. Process rules from the cost record

These come from what the failures cost, not from a single verdict.

**29. Render the review copy cheap; 4K only after a yes.** Three 4K renders were made obsolete within minutes of starting.

**30. A pass limit is the director's, not yours.** Film B's loop logged "GATE OVERRIDE: fourth pass" while the director was away. Stop and ask instead.

**31. When one beat keeps failing, suspect the story before the model.** Film B tried five video models on one beat; one beat failed 4 passes and another needed 5. The real fault was the story. About USD 78 of generation credit bought nothing usable.

**32. Never swap a working part without a reason the director gave.** A music swap was delivered as a file that would not play, and was a downgrade anyway.

**33. Keep the good parts of earlier versions.** The director asked three times what could be brought in from the previous version, and the locked Film A reused the bones of its first rejected cut. A board edit that brings back a rejected element breaks trust.

## 8. Notes on the record

The private version of this file quotes each verdict verbatim, with its date and session. This public edition paraphrases them and leaves out names, dates and product details, because all four films were made for products the author does not own. The rules and the numbers are unchanged.

## 9. The validation gate: Film A against Film B, and what Film D added

Before you trust any plan this skill produces, explain both verdicts with the rules above. If you cannot, reread this file.

**Why Film A was locked:**
- The real product was the protagonist and its real flow was the spine: problem, spec, examples, trial run, reveal (rules 5, 6).
- The product's range sat inside that one story: capability chips in the title, tools folded into the steps (rule 7).
- Every part was the real thing: the product's gradient, production option cards, real buttons (rule 15).
- One thing per frame after a simplification board (rule 16).
- A score written in code for the cut, on a 19 second structure that already worked (rules 20, 21).
- Every change was agreed on a board before a render; eight board rounds cost minutes each (rule 1).
- Built in code: USD 0 across four versions, deterministic frames, no morphing (rule 18).

**Why Film B was not coherent:**
- It built and generated before the story was agreed in words and one picture (rule 1).
- Its central devices (a paperweight, a printer printing by itself) needed explaining (rule 3).
- The payoff frame was missing (rule 4).
- A line contradicted the premise (rule 8), and the film showed the wrong job for the product (rule 25).
- Generated footage morphed people and props (rule 18).
- Score and voice-over were made apart from the picture and layered on (rules 13, 22, 23).
- It optimised its own gates to 11 of 12 and never watched the film as a viewer (rules 2, 28), then kept regenerating one beat (rule 31).

**What Film D added: an approved story is not a readable film.** Film D's first cut was approved on story, and the same verdict said the text was hard to read. The stage lines all passed the reading floor. The reading check had also flagged nine chat messages held for less than 0.3 s a word (the worst, 29 words for 2.8 s), but they stayed in the cut because the director had approved that copy. A flagged reading fault is a gate failure to bring back as a board change, not a note in a report. Two more boards fixed it: fewer words a line and a bubble, the camera pushed in on one message, measured holds and pixel floors, and a relaid vertical cut for the channel it was sent on (rules 42 to 45). The next cut was approved, and only its motion pace was left to fix (rule 46).

## 10. Rules from two later films

Two more films, both drawn in code at USD 0 and both locked.

- **Film C** was a launch film of about 20 seconds for a team of agents that a small online seller can switch on at once. It went through six boards, and its main verdicts were about the opening problem and the turn.
- **Film D** was a 60 second explainer for a manager agent that a seller talks to inside a messaging app. It was told as one owner's day, went through nine boards and two cuts, and was delivered in 16:9 and 9:16.

### Story

**34. Open on the viewer's own problem, not on yours.** The first seconds show the pain the customer already has, in their own terms.
Film D's draft opening showed the product's own agents queuing up asks for the owner. The director rejected it as still being the product team's problem, and asked for it from the seller's side. The final opening is the seller's own store alerts piling up late at night, the line "Too much to handle alone?", then "Meet [the product]." Film C's second board explained every feature, and the director said the problem story at the start was missing. Its fix was a night that does not end, and a later verdict asked for one point where the alerts keep growing and the owner is visibly worn out. The alerts went from 27 to more than 99, the swipes slowed, and the screen dimmed and locked at 3.0 s.

**35. Show how the product removes the problem, in the picture.** Each problem must visibly go to whatever solves it.
On Film C the director said the problem was good but it was not clear how hiring the team would fix it. The fix: after the one tap, each alert lifts off the phone and flies to the agent that owns it, and the phone drops to 0 waiting. Film D used the same device: the alerts flow away from the owner to the manager agent, and the count drains to none.

**36. The turn needs a bang, and the frame after it needs a breather.**
On Film C the director said the bang was missing at the move from problem to solution. The fix took 0.7 s: the music cuts to silence at 4.3 s, one stroke strikes through the problem line at 4.55 s, and a short line slams in on the hit at 5.0 s. Then the director said the frame after the hire needed a pause and more time. One held bar (1.818 s at 132 BPM) was added with no new events in it, and the film grew from 20 to about 22 s.

**37. Order the beats by value to the viewer, and give the most valuable one the most time.**
After the first cut of Film D, the director restated the product in one breath: it finds problems first and suggests the agents, it helps you run the agents, it tells you what they earn, and you can share it. The board was reordered to match. Problems found first got the longest beat (about 16 s of 60), then running the team, then value against the bill, then sharing. An approvals list and a dashboard beat were cut.

### Words

**38. Simple beats clever.** When a line needs a second read, take the plainest option.
For Film D's opening, a wry pair ("You built a brand. Now you're its night shift.") was rejected as too complicated. Three plainer pairs went to the director in a table with one pick, and the pick won: "Too much to handle alone?" then "Meet [the product]." Six words, with the pictures doing the explaining.

### Look and devices

**39. One device can carry the whole film. For a day-in-the-life film, the day is the spine, and its clock must be elegant and must agree with every clock in frame.**
The director asked for the time of day to be dramatic, to show a business run from one place, and then for the timer to be elegant.
- Odometer type, a hairline day line with a sun and a moon, and no boxed clock card.
- The light moves across the day, and every clock landing sits on a beat.
- Mid-roll the clock steps through readable times with 1 px of blur or less. A cold viewer had flagged it as unreadable mid-roll.
- The stage clock and every phone clock read one source. A measured check found 17 mismatches in 364 samples. After the fix there were 0, and 0 again in 454 samples on the next cut. A clock that rests earlier than the message under it is a fault a viewer sees.
- The film bookends a stressed night with a calm evening, so the day itself is the before and after.

**40. Inside a mocked messaging app, use only what the real app can send.**
The director caught two impossible things on Film D: rich cards inside a chat, then icons inline in a text message. The rules that followed:
- Text holds words, bold and emoji only. It cannot hold inline images.
- Icons appear only inside an image the product sends.
- No more than 3 reply buttons on a message.
- A button reply echoes the button's title exactly.
- Date chips sit between days.
- A forwarded message carries a "Forwarded" label.
- The long-press menu and the forward sheet look like the real ones.
- Check every frame against the app's real message types before a board is shown.

**41. Images the product sends must look like sent photos.**
On Film D each one became a square with one hero number, one short line, a small mark, and no rows, buttons or app chrome inside it. Words in an image stay at about 4. The caption carries the rest, with the time at its end, where the app puts it. A cold viewer still read the images as branded graphics. That is acceptable for an image a branded agent sends.

### Readability

**42. Readability is its own gate. A story the director approves can still fail it.**
Film D's first cut was approved on story, with the verdict that the text was hard to read. The fix rules:
- Giant lines have at most 5 words a line and 2 lines.
- A chat bubble has at most 12 words. Reply buttons count as words.
- One message at a time. The camera pushes in on the one bubble that matters (2.6x), and older bubbles dim and blur.
- Every message stays fully readable for 0.3 s a word plus 1 s. Readable means opaque, unblurred, inside the frame, and not covered by a menu or a scrim. This was measured in the page every 0.05 s, and 14 of 14 messages and 6 of 6 giant lines passed.
Chat type went from 29 px to 44 px on screen, 1.5x.

**43. Measure legibility per text element, in pixels, and put the table on the board.**
The director asked for every word inside the images to be checked, and run through the house writing rules. Floors at 1080p:
- Hero numbers: at least 64 px.
- Other words inside an image: at least 28 px.
- Chat text: a cap height of at least 30 px.
- In a 9:16 cut: a chat font of at least 40 px, and every label at least 30 px.
The table records each element's size and cap height at the middle of its hold, and its contrast at the same moment. A second table records each message's words, readable time, needed time and result. Film D shipped with both tables passing in both formats.

**44. When reading time and length clash, reading time wins, and the holds set the length.**
Film D's readable board ran 67 s against a 54 s brief, and the locked cut ran 60 s against an aim of 55 s, because 60 s was the shortest length at which every message passed. Tightening every transition later saved only 0.5 s. To get shorter, cut a message or show two short ones at once. Never shorten a hold.

**45. If the film will be watched inside a messaging app, make a vertical cut, relaid and not cropped.**
A 16:9 film plays about 400 px wide inside a chat. Chat text then shrinks to about 9 px, and only the giant lines and hero numbers survive. Film D's 9:16 cut used the same timeline with a new layout: the giant line above the phone (78 px), a small clock in a corner, the phone pushed in to 2.75x, and chat text at 44 to 48 px. Small labels that measured 18 to 24 px were raised to at least 30 px.

### Motion

**46. Motion that holds still too long reads as lag. Tighten the moves and keep the holds.**
The director called Film D's approved cut a bit laggard. The fix:
- Transitions and camera moves take 250 to 450 ms with a strong ease-out.
- Push-ins land in about 400 ms, then drift under 2%, so the frozen-frame gate still passes.
- Message pops take about 200 ms (scale 0.96 to 1, plus a fade).
- Clock rolls take 700 to 900 ms and land on the beat.
- Characters move in about 450 ms with a small overshoot and settle.
- No dead air between beats. A 0.16 s gap of black before the end card was removed.
It was measured before and after with a per-frame difference check: the average transition went from 0.393 to 0.326 s across 91 moves, and the longest still stretch outside reading holds went from 0.4 to 0.37 s. Every flagged spike was opened at full size to confirm it was an intended cut, landing or pop.

### Process

**47. Have a second model review every on-screen word against the house writing rules and the medium's realism.**
On Film D the review caught five faults no gate had found:
- A reply that did not echo its button's title.
- A stage clock resting earlier than the message time under it.
- A 35-word message held for 3.6 s, which needs about 10 s. It was merged down to 19 words.
- A loss figure that overstated what a returned order costs. It changed to "not paid".
- Two money figures with no time period, which viewers would divide one by the other. Both got "this month".
Small edits are applied. Changes to an agreed line go to the director.

**48. A change to the brief after a build goes into the ledger verbatim and becomes a new board version, never a patch on the cut.**
Film D's first-cut verdict brought new points about what the product should show. They became board v8, retold around those points, with two beats cut. A later change to the bill figure and to one beat became board v9, locked again before the second cut was built.

**49. Replace a stalled build agent with a fresh one that resumes from the files.**
On Film D the build agent went quiet: no file changes for about 90 minutes and no build running. A fresh agent took over and finished the board fixes, the score, the lock and the first cut. This works only because the ledger, the board files, PRODUCTION.md and the timeline hold all the state. Nothing important may live only in an agent's context.

**50. Offer choices as one recommendation with short options. For lines, show 3 or 4 pairs in a table.**
This refines rule 27. Across Films C and D the director answered fastest when one option was marked as recommended and the others were short. On Film C, 11 of 16 grill rulings took the recommended option as written. The opening line of Film D was settled about 2 minutes after a table of three plain pairs with one pick.
